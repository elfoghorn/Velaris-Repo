II.

History

She was born twenty years into a war that had already forgotten peace. Adonai-Vel raised its children fast, and for one purpose. Nobody born with her blood got a soft year in that city. Her people were losing ground in those first two decades, taking what small victories they could while they learned, one battle at a time, that heaven's might couldn't actually be matched. By the time she could walk, they'd stopped fighting to win and started praying for a miracle to turn the tide.

She held a wooden practice sword before she could write her own name. By six she was sparring nine-year-olds and losing badly, and the beatings taught her something useful: how to take pain and keep going, the way a soldier has to. By twelve she was winning, and the instructors started watching her. After that: more drills, more hours, older soldiers to lose against, again and again, until the losing stopped.

Nobody asked if she wanted any of it. Nobody needed to. Her eyes said everything a recruiter would have wanted to hear anyway: a killer, honed young. The war wanted soldiers and it was going to get them, one exhausted, bruised, relentless child at a time, and she turned out good at becoming exactly what it needed.

One drill-master kept her back after a brutal training session, watching her get back up faster than anyone else in her cohort. He could see something in the way she moved, the way she fought: that she was ready to stop holding her two halves apart and let them run together, the thing most Nephilim spent their whole lives avoiding, as if mixing the blood was a blight rather than a gift. The strongest ones learned to embrace it anyway. He started keeping her back in the evenings after that, drilling her in it, pushing her further as the two sides of her blood learned to move as one.

Once she stopped fighting it, it came to her easier than breathing. Her officers noticed and started dragging younger soldiers over to watch her spar, less as praise than as a threat: this is possible, so stop being lazy. The nobles called it a blight all the same and treated her accordingly, even as their own children never came within reach of matching her.

She spent her twenties, thirties, and forties in the unglamorous parts of the war nobody wrote down: years-long sieges against angelic positions, border raids, river crossings held a night longer than they should have held. A retreat she covered alone at thirty-one bought a column of wounded soldiers enough time to reach a treeline. She walked out of the ambush two hours later drenched in blood that wasn't hers, breathing hard, something closer to bloodlust than relief still sitting in her eyes. The soldiers she'd just saved watched her pass in silence. Nobody thanked her for that one.

She won more than she lost. In a hundred-year war against something that doesn't tire, that's already close to a miracle. By eighty she commanded a full regiment and held a rank the nobles could no longer argue with, whatever they still privately called what had earned it for her.

Then came the last push.

Heaven came at the capital from every direction at once, more of them than any drill had prepared the wall's watch to expect. Marasa'n's regiment held a flank just outside the walls: the unglamorous work of keeping one stretch of a dying line from folding while the people who mattered defended the throne room further in. The first wave broke before the horns finished sounding, ranks of angels advancing behind shields rimmed in white fire, wings folded tight against their backs to cut the wind resistance of a full run.

She met the front rank at the ditch line rather than waiting for it to reach the earthworks, closing the gap before their formation had finished setting. Her first swing took the lead angel through the collarbone before his shield came fully up. The second attack came from a blade she never saw start its swing, and she read it in his shoulders a half-second before his arm moved, the same tell she'd been trained to watch since she was twelve, and turned it aside on the flat of her blade rather than losing time to a full parry. She didn't stop moving. Standing still in that kind of press was its own kind of dying.

Her style kept the angels guessing: Vashtara's weight went into every blow that connected, enough force behind an ordinary greatsword swing to put an angel down through his own shield. Celestine's speed carried her between them faster than armored infantry could track, closing a gap, taking an opening, moving again before a second attacker could commit. Angels who'd trained their whole lives against one kind of magic or the other kept flinching at the wrong half a half-second too late. That half-second was the whole fight, most days. They hadn't expected it either. Almost nobody outside Adonai-Vel ever did.

An hour in, a knight-captain broke off from the second wave and came for her personally, marked out by the soldiers around her who'd started giving her more room than the line could actually afford. He fought clean and fast, good enough that she had to actually work for the first time since the ditch line, and he nearly opened her left arm on a reverse cut she read a beat too late. Her magic answered before she consciously called it, pulsing through a single swing of raw, indiscriminate force that cut clean through the knight-captain and tore a hole through one of his entourage standing behind him.

Soldiers fell on both sides of her all afternoon, hers more than she wanted and theirs enough to matter. She held ground rather than gained it, which was the entire assignment, and she held it past the point her arms should have given out, past three separate wounds that should have pulled her out of the line entirely. Command had told her to keep the flank from folding for as long as the throne room needed her to. She didn't know how long that would be. She simply didn't stop. Her flank held to the end. It just stopped mattering by the time it did.

The Heavens' Final Descent.

She was close enough to see it happen. Light thrown down from a ruptured sky, more of it than any single defense could survive, striking the heart of a nation that had spent a hundred years convincing itself it had earned the right to stand as heaven's equal. She watched her capital die from just outside its own walls, close enough that the light caught the edge of her vision and told her exactly what she was looking at before her mind had finished processing it.

She spent the next two days pulling survivors out of rubble that used to be a city, working past the point where her body should have given out. Nothing could have kept her still after watching her civilization's final breath snuffed out in a torrent of light from the heavens.

Two days became weeks. Someone had to get the survivors moving away from a ruin that could still kill them with a strong wind and a bad slope, and there was nobody left with a higher rank willing to do it, so she took the mantle herself. She turned a scattering of burned, starving, directionless people into something that functioned: watch rotations through the night, water parties sent to whatever streams hadn't been fouled, a rough count of the living kept in her own head because there was no clerk left alive to keep it properly. She gave orders to people who'd outranked her a month earlier, because giving orders was the only thing keeping either of them upright.

Nobody organized the exile that followed. It didn't need organizing. What was left of Adonai-Vel had nowhere left to be a nation, and once her makeshift camp had held long enough for people to manage on their own, its survivors walked away from the wreckage in every direction. Whatever hope any of them had left, they'd decided it lay elsewhere. Regrouping meant risking heaven's attention a second time, and none of them were willing to test whether it would come back. They stayed broken and scattered instead, and called it survival.

She didn't let herself grieve while any of that was happening. There wasn't room for it. Grief was a thing you did once everyone else was safe, and everyone else kept not being safe for months at a stretch, so she postponed it the same way she'd been taught to postpone exhaustion and hunger and pain since she was old enough to hold a blade. By the time the last of her people had scattered far enough to manage without her, there was nobody left standing close enough to notice her finally stop.

She howled for weeks after that, watching what was left of her city still burning, while everyone with the sense left to do it had already gone to gather up whatever remained of their lives. Her isolation from the rest of the bloodline took years to fully settle in. It was years more before she found her own way to carry Adonai-Vel forward, not by rebuilding it, but by holding onto whatever pieces of it she could still find and keep.

Not once, in nine hundred years.

What she did in the months after, she doesn't talk about either. What matters is that she survived it, and the survival came with nothing attached. No home to return to. No throne to serve. Just a very long life, suddenly, stretching out in front of her with nothing in it worth naming.

Two centuries passed before she found anything that felt like it belonged to her again.

She wasn't looking for it, which still makes her laugh on the rare occasions she's honest about this memory. She'd been drifting through the empty places her people left behind, half grief and half boredom, when she found the ruin of a military outpost so forgotten even the war had lost track of it. It fell early, decades before the fighting reached the capital, and nobody had touched it since.

Buried under what used to be an armory roof, she found a blade unlike anything she'd carried before. It answered the moment her hand closed around the grip, shifting between a light form and a dark form depending on which half of her she called into it, the metal itself changing color rather than just some glow sitting on top of it. She named it after the only word from the old tongue she considered worthy of its history.

Vel'kaesh. It hasn't left her side since. She has never fully explained, even to herself, why finding it felt like coming home when nothing else had come close in two hundred years.

The centuries after that blur together on purpose. She tells herself that's just what happens to memory once there's enough of it to carry, and maybe that's even true. What's certain is that she kept moving, kept fighting, kept chasing anything Adonai-Vel had left scattered across the world, and built a trail of half-true legends behind her without ever meaning to.

The Docks

The docks came first, in a fishing town whose name she's let herself forget. Raiders hit before dawn, the kind after boats, stores, and whatever coin the harbor kept in its one strongbox. Half the crew was already ashore before anyone raised an alarm. She was passing through on a hunch about an old trade route, sleeping rough near the water, and the noise woke her before the first scream did.

She skipped the alarm bell and the town watch and went straight for the boats. Whatever the raiders expected that night, it wasn't a woman walking out of the dark with a blade already drawn, closing the distance before any of them finished drawing steel. By the time the watch arrived, there was nothing left to do. What they found afterward didn't read as a fight so much as a lesson: wounds cut with a precision that had nothing frantic about it, for all how fast it must have happened.

She refused every reward the harbor master offered and asked one question: did anyone recognize a design carved into a chest the raiders had been hauling toward their ship. Nobody did. She checked it herself, confirmed it wasn't what she was looking for, and was gone before sunrise. The sailors still tell it as a ghost story more than a war story. She was never quite either.

The Mountain Road

The mountain road came next, months later and a long way inland. A trade caravan was crossing a pass that had held for a hundred years without incident, the same afternoon Marasa'n happened to be crossing it too. The slope let go all at once: rock and loose scree, enough weight to bury the whole caravan before anyone could turn the horses.

She didn't outrun it. She planted herself between the caravan and the slope, drove Vashtara's half into the ground through both palms, and held the collapse long enough for it to matter. Miners still argue about what they watched: some say the rockface simply stopped, others that the stone flowed around her rather than through her. Either way, nobody in that caravan died, and all of them owed it to a stranger who hadn't said a word since she arrived.

She held the pass open until the caravan cleared it, then let the rest of the slope fall behind them, sealing the road she'd now have to find another way around. Nobody caught her name, just the sword she carried, unlike any smithing the local miners had seen, gone before anyone thought to ask where she was headed.

The Antiques Shop

The antiques shop came third, in a market town with more secondhand relics passing through it than any dealer could properly authenticate. Marasa'n found a dented old gauntlet in a bin priced as scrap, old Adonai-Vel make if she'd ever seen it, and paid the asking price without haggling. The dealer remembers being surprised. Most customers argued on principle.

She came back a week later, not to buy. She'd found two more pieces from the same set among other collectors, obvious forgeries once she'd handled the genuine article, both sold out of his shop under the same provenance. She laid it out for him calmly, without raising her voice, which made the conversation worse than shouting would have.

He hasn't sold a fake since, not entirely out of fear. He tells people the pale-eyed woman who came back that week knew more about Adonai-Vel relics than any scholar he's dealt with, and that he'd rather lose a sale than see her a third time. He still has no idea who she was.

The Border Garrison

The border garrison came last. By the time word of it traveled, most details had gone strange in the retelling. What's agreed on: a garrison under siege for six days, walls holding but supplies not, went to sleep on the seventh night still surrounded and woke on the eighth with its gates standing open from the inside.

Every officer commanding the siege lay dead, slaughtered in their sleep, killed quietly enough that the camp around them never woke. Nobody at the garrison could explain how one person got through a siege line, did that much damage, and got out again before dawn. The one surviving account describes a woman standing just inside the gate at first light, greatsword sheathed, waiting only long enough to confirm the garrison would hold before walking back out through the gap she'd made.

She asked for nothing. She gave no name, no explanation, and no time for anyone to organize the thanks the garrison spent the next week trying to arrange. By the time a rider caught up to the road she'd taken out of the valley, there was no one left on it to thank.

None of them know it was the same woman.

None of them know she's still out there, still doing more or less the same thing, for more or less the same reasons she started.

Somewhere in those centuries she stopped counting something else too: the people she let herself get close to, and how fast she had to watch them go. Ten years reads to her like a stopover, barely long enough to wear in new boots. She's watched it swallow a mortal's entire life, first steps to last breath, while she was still unpacking.

A friend made over a shared fire and a shared fight could be dead of old age before she'd finished the errand that brought her to their town. A companion she genuinely loved could live a full life beside her, marry, raise children, grow old and grey, and be gone before she'd started to feel the years pass. She learned, slowly and at real cost, to love people anyway, and to watch every one of them go.

She doesn't talk about that either, though it explains more about her than the war does. Most days it's easier to keep moving before anyone gets close enough to become one more name she'll have to outlive. It's also exactly why she keeps doing it anyway. Standing still long enough to build something with someone means agreeing to bury them, and she still hasn't decided if that's a price worth paying or a debt she's too stubborn to stop taking.

She arrived in Velaris like she arrives everywhere: quietly, with nothing waiting for her, and no plan to stay.

Fifty-odd years into her time there, she did something she hadn't managed anywhere else in eight centuries. She decided to make a home.

She opened a shop in the capital, a narrow storefront a few doors down from the portal crossing to Auralith, and started selling what centuries of hunting relics had actually taught her: how to appreciate and evaluate what's real. Half-Light Antiquities deals in provenance she can verify herself, instruments, jewelry, ledgers, the occasional weapon or piece of armor too well made for the century it's dated to. Wealthy collectors from both sides of the portal find their way to her counter eventually. What never crosses it, no matter the offer, is anything that was actually Adonai-Vel's. Those pieces she keeps in rooms above the shop that no customer has ever seen.

She still leaves for months at a time when a real lead surfaces, locking the door and trusting the street not to ask questions it already knows better than asking. She always comes back. Fifty years is nothing against nine centuries, but it's the longest she's kept anything in one place since the Hollowing, and she still isn't entirely sure what to make of that.

What she's noticed, and hasn't said aloud to anyone, is that it's easier than she expected. A shop doesn't age the way a friend does. She can care about the work, even about the handful of regular customers who've become something like familiar to her, without the arithmetic that usually stops her from staying anywhere at all. It doesn't replace what she lost. It's the closest she's let herself get to keeping something since.

III.

How She Fights

She fights the way Adonai-Vel trained her to: forward, fast, and built to end things quickly. She closes distance immediately and keeps pressing until the fight is over. Centuries removed from the war, this is still the only way she fights.

She uses a wide, low stance built for a two-handed blade that outweighs most opponents' entire kit, and keeps her weight forward rather than back, so a parry becomes the start of her next swing instead of a stop. She reads an opponent's hips and shoulders rather than their weapon hand: a feint shows in the hips first, and a real strike shows in the shoulders a half-second before the arm moves. Against a group, she holds a position where her blade's arc threatens two opponents at once and times her swings to force the rest into each other's space.

Vel'kaesh is heavier than a blade her size should manage one-handed for long, and she compensates with rotation rather than raw arm strength: full-body turns that carry the next swing off the last one's momentum instead of muscling through it fresh. That technique works with or without magic behind it. What her magic adds on top of it is force beyond her own muscle, speed beyond her frame's normal limits, and enough continued output to keep fighting through injuries that would stop her otherwise.

Five Ways She Channels It
Weight of the Fall

She channels Vashtara's half directly into a single strike as it lands, adding force her muscles alone can't generate. It cuts through plate and warded steel a normal blow wouldn't scratch. The edge darkens for the instant the strike connects, then clears again.

Herald's Speed

She channels Celestine's half into her legs for a two-to-three-step burst, closing distance faster than an opponent can reset their stance. She uses it to open an exchange. It costs her almost nothing to trigger and rarely runs longer than the first three swings need.

Umbral Step

She channels Vashtara's half into a single step timed to the exact moment a blow would land, shifting her position enough that the strike passes through the space she just left. It replaces a dodge or a parry outright. She uses it against a hit she has no other way of clearing in time.

Seraph's Ward

She channels Celestine's half into a flat wall of light angled to catch one incoming attack rather than shield her from every side. It absorbs that one blow directly, and costs her more to hold than Umbral Step does, so she raises it only when stepping clear isn't possible.

Stubborn Mending

She keeps a low, constant channel of Celestine's half running under an open wound, slowing the bleeding and holding an otherwise fight-ending injury at a level she can keep moving on. It doesn't close the wound or restore anything she's already lost. It buys her time before the wound needs real treatment.

Two She Holds in Reserve
The Halo Fall

She channels as much of Celestine's half as she can hold at once, enough that the golden halo forms fully behind her head, then releases all of it down Vel'kaesh's edge in a single overhead strike. The strike carries the same kind of concentrated light the Seraphim used to end Adonai-Vel, at a far smaller scale. Holding that much of Celestine's half open leaves her exposed for the two or three seconds it takes to release it, which is why she uses it rarely. Whatever it hits, it ends.

Vashtara's Reckoning

She channels Vashtara's half at full output and holds the channel open instead of releasing it in one strike, shifting Vel'kaesh fully into its dark form and driving a continuous series of full-weight blows rather than a single one, each landing heavier than the last as the channel builds. She uses it once precision has already failed and the fight calls for sustained damage over accuracy.

The light that leaves Vel'kaesh's edge in that moment is bright enough to end whatever it hits outright. She's spent nearly three-quarters of her reserve the moment it lands, win or lose. She doesn't call on it unless the fight has already gone past the point normal abilities can settle it.

Weaknesses: The two-post charge is the longest window she has, and she's essentially defenseless while building it. Leaves her running on fumes afterward regardless of whether it connects. Six-post cooldown means she can't use it again for the rest of most fights.

She lets Vashtara's half build instead of releasing it, shifting Vel'kaesh fully into its dark form and swinging without pause for accuracy. Each blow in the sequence lands heavier than the last as the channel keeps escalating underneath it. She commits everything to landing those blows and takes a hit in return she has no way to defend against while it runs. By the third post, the sequence has done enough damage that whatever's left standing rarely stays that way for long. Once it ends, the built-up force releases all at once, and she has nothing left to call on again for six posts.

Weaknesses: Offers essentially no defense of her own while the sequence runs. Interrupting her early, before the force builds, denies most of the technique's power. Six-post cooldown and heavy draw make it a once-per-fight commitment.

A companion she traveled with for a decade shows the years plainly by the time she sees them again. She hasn't visibly aged at all since long before that decade started. It costs her nothing to maintain, and she rarely thinks about it unless someone else brings it up first.

VI.

The Relic She Carries

Vel'kaesh

Recovered from a lost outpost, two hundred years after the Hollowing

Adonai-Vel's smiths worked gold and dark silver into the same pieces the way its architects did, but most of that work never went past the surface. A small caste of smiths, never more than a handful of names at once, learned to infuse both magics into the metal during the forging itself. Vel'kaesh is one of the last true examples of that craft still standing anywhere.

It answers whichever half of her inheritance she calls on, shifting form rather than resisting it. Ordinary dual-worked steel fatigues under that kind of contradiction and eventually fails. Vel'kaesh releases the stress as transformation instead of damage. It has never once failed her, in a career built on the assumption that it eventually will.

She named it after the only word from the old tongue she considered worthy of its history. No smith she's found since, in Velaris or anywhere past it, can explain how the transformation works. She's had over six centuries to decide that not understanding it isn't the same as not trusting it.

VII.

Half-Light Antiquities

A little over fifty years into her time in Velaris, she did something she hadn't managed anywhere else in eight centuries. She decided to make a home.

Location
Velaris Capital, near the Auralith portal crossing
Specialty
Verified old-world antiques — never Adonai-Vel's own
Runs the Counter
Perrin Oswitt, in her absence

Half-Light Antiquities deals in provenance she can verify herself: instruments, jewelry, ledgers, the occasional weapon or piece of armor too well made for the century it's dated to. Wealthy collectors from both sides of the portal find their way to her counter eventually. What never crosses it, no matter the offer, is anything that was actually Adonai-Vel's. Those pieces she keeps in rooms above the shop that no customer has ever seen. She keeps loose, practical ties to a few of the couriers working Auralith's Underground Tunnels too, since black-market relic traffic reaches her faster through them than through any honest auction house.

She still leaves for months at a time when a real lead surfaces, locking the door and trusting the street not to ask questions it already knows better than asking. She always comes back. Fifty years is nothing against nine centuries, but it's the longest she's kept anything in one place since the Hollowing.

What she's noticed, and hasn't said aloud to anyone, is that it's easier than she expected. A shop doesn't age the way a friend does. She can care about the work, even about the handful of regular customers who've become something like familiar to her, without the arithmetic that usually stops her from staying anywhere at all. It doesn't replace what she lost. It's the closest she's let herself get to keeping something since.

VIII.

What She Hasn't Said

Held, Not Played

Traces of Adonai-Vel, wherever they surface, are what keep her moving. She hasn't told anyone in Velaris yet, but she already knows something most of the Empire doesn't: the ruin the Hollowlands call the Precipice Realm, Ashura's ruin, the one with the Late King's abandoned study and the unexplained Hell Gates, is Adonai-Vel. Her Adonai-Vel. She was there the day it happened. She hasn't decided whether to say so, to whom, or what she wants from confirming it beyond the confirmation itself.

  • Unresolved on whether Adonai-Vel deserved the Hollowing. Hasn't decided, and dislikes being asked, which is a harder question for her than for most survivors since she was one of the soldiers fighting to keep it standing.
  • Has not set foot on that ground since the day she survived it. Knows the ruin is close now that she's in Velaris. Hasn't gone.
  • No one in Velaris currently has a reason to connect her to Ashura's ruin. She holds that card without having played it.
  • Doesn't know that the woman signing off on Hearthstone District's rent prices as "the Empress" accounts for her bloodline's existence. No one has told her. No one currently could.
  • Fifty years is the longest she's stayed anywhere since the Hollowing. She hasn't examined too closely why Velaris, of all places, is the one that stuck.
  • Perrin Oswitt doesn't know what she's actually looking for on her trips, only that he's paid well enough not to ask. She hasn't decided whether that's kindness or convenience.
  • The old tongue isn't dead. Somewhere, a handful of lineage-keepers still speak it whole and guard the teaching of it as fiercely as anything Adonai-Vel has left. She isn't one of them, has never found one, and doesn't know if she'd be trusted to learn from one if she did.
  • The nobility's "blight" verdict on her mixed magic never got a real reckoning. Adonai-Vel fell before anyone had to answer for it, and she's never decided whether that counts as unfinished business or just one more thing the Hollowing buried along with everything else.
